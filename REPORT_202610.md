# Cantonese Jyutping recognition: run21 results and error analysis (October 2026)

Branch `neclueus-tone`. Model: `facebook/w2v-bert-2.0`-based encoder with one CTC head over onset and rime+tone tokens.
Report date 2026-10-10. Numbers are single-seed.

## Summary

- **run21 is the best model so far.** It starts from the published model's encoder and trains on `tts20250516` plus the
  train split of `dataset_emo_jyutping` (300,886 clips, 40,000 steps, 1 h 51 min on one H100). Final checkpoint
  (`checkpoint-40000`): **1.53% syllable error rate (SER) on the fixed 500-clip test split**, 1.71% on the emo test split,
  and **12 errors in the 79 syllables of the 6 hand-labelled wavs** (run20: 1.75% / 3.05% / 17; published model: 5.24% / - / 14).
  The gains over run20 are small (about 10 syllables on the test split, 5 on the wavs); only their direction is consistent.
- **Tone is the weakest part of the model.** It is wrong in 1.0-1.3% of syllables, against 0.2-0.35% for onsets and 0.27% for
  rimes, and makes up 65-76% of all substitutions. About 60% of the tone errors are the pairs 3/6, 4/6 and 2/5.
- **Half of all in-domain errors differ from the gold only by a merger pair** (tone 3/6, 4/6, 2/5; onset n/l, ng/-, gw/g).
  Without them the SER would be 0.78% (tts) and 0.89% (emo). The labels are not human annotation (see "Label
  provenance" below), so part of this is a labelling issue, not a recognition failure.
- **Label provenance changes how every number here reads.** Both datasets were labelled by previously trained models
  with ToJyutping (a text-to-jyutping converter) as the reference. The in-domain SER is therefore agreement with that
  pseudo-labelling, not accuracy against what was said, and only the 6 hand-checked wavs measure the latter. Measured:
  the labels differ from ToJyutping(`text`) in 3.0% (tts) and 5.0% (emo) of syllables, and about half of the model's
  errors sit exactly on those positions (section 2, "Label provenance check").
- **A pitch check of the tone disagreements** (section 2, context-aware tone classifier, 69.6% on six tones) suggested the
  audio fits the model's tone in about 63% of the checkable cases, but **it failed its validation on the person-verified
  wavs**: of the 5 model tone errors there, pitch sided with the verified label once. The classifier is a calibrated tone
  estimate when confident (95% at confidence 0.9 and above), but its errors are correlated with the model's, so it cannot
  judge model-versus-label disagreements and the 63% is probably overstated. A person has to verify those by ear.
- **A lexical (text-based) route was tested and not supported:** the true characters with the labellers' own lexicon
  reproduce the labels worse (2.0-3.1%) than the model does (1.5-1.7%), and a character-LM decoder over the model's tone
  posteriors removes about 11% of the merger-pair errors with no net gain (section 2).
- **Rare syllables are the second weakness.** Syllables seen fewer than 1,000 times in training are 8-11% of the tokens
  and 29-45% of the errors.
- **run21 is not fitted to one dataset** (section 2, cross-dataset check): onset, rime and tone error rates, confusion pairs
  and token weaknesses agree between tts and emo, and its training-clip error (1.1-1.5%) is close to its held-out error.
  The dataset-dependent part is tone, then rime: a tts-only model's tone errors are 2.5 times higher on emo.
- **The 6-wav errors are mostly a different kind from the in-domain errors**: m->b, d->z, p->b and g->f never occur in the
  held-out sets. The wav gap is therefore not explained by the label conventions, but 79 syllables is too few to be sure.
- **Post-processing with a syllable language model is not a reliable fix** (section 4.1): validation-tuned rescoring
  changes the test errors by -9% (not significant), makes the emo test slightly worse, and leaves the merger-pair errors
  untouched. The correct sentence is in the n-best almost always (oracle SER 0.1%), so a better reranker could still help.
- Serving now defaults to `checkpoints/run21_40000`; the ONNX export matches PyTorch exactly on 156 clips.

## 1. The model and how it was trained

| | run20 | run21 |
|---|---|---|
| Encoder start | `facebook/w2v-bert-2.0` | published `hon9kon9ize/wav2vec2bert-jyutping` (fresh single head) |
| Training data | `tts20250516` train (260,524 clips) | the same + `dataset_emo_jyutping` train (40,362) = 300,886 clips |
| Recipe | encoder lr 5e-5, head lr 1e-3, batch 16, warmup 100, bf16 | the same |
| Steps | 30,000 | 40,000 (2.13 epochs) |
| Speed and cost | ~7 steps/s, 1 h 16 min on H100 | ~6 steps/s, 1 h 51 min on H100 (c-batch, job 7042053) |

A 300-step pilot on 6,000 mixed clips gave 4.6% validation SER (gate: below 30%) before the full run started.
Validation SER (500 clips) during the run: 4.13% (step 2k), 3.01% (6k), 2.27% (12k), 2.06% (20k), 1.64% (34k),
1.41% (36k, best), 1.43% (38k), 1.47% (40k). SER was flat around 2.3% from step 12k to 18k, and the late drop came with
the learning-rate decay.

### Results

Test = the fixed 500-clip `tts20250516` split. emo test = `dataset_emo_jyutping`'s 1,035-clip test split (13,344
syllables). Wavs = the 6 hand-labelled clips (79 syllables, labels corrected against the audio).

| Model | Test SER | emo test SER | 6 wavs (79 syll.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old full data (`checkpoint-3048`) | 1.75% | 3.41% | 20 errors (25.3%) |
| run20 (base init) | 1.75% | 3.05% | 17 (21.5%) |
| run21 `checkpoint-36000` (best by validation) | 1.60% | 1.83% | 13 (16.5%) |
| **run21 `checkpoint-40000` (final)** | **1.53%** | **1.71%** | **12 (15.2%)** |

- The two run21 checkpoints differ by a few syllables, which is noise; the final one is used because it needs no
  selection step.
- The emo test is not clean for run21: its encoder comes from the model that trained on that dataset. The published
  model's 0.01% on it is memorisation, not a fair yardstick.
- No paired significance test was run for run21 against run20. Treat the gaps as consistent in direction, not proven.

## 2. Error analysis

### Method

- Checkpoint-40000 decoded at batch size 1 with the length-aware CTC decode over three held-out sets: tts test (500 clips),
  tts validation (500 clips; the trainer used it to pick its best checkpoint, 36000, which is not the checkpoint
  analysed here) and emo test (1,035 clips). The two tts
  sets are pooled as "tts" (9,250 gold syllables); emo has 13,344.
- Gold and predicted syllable sequences are aligned by edit distance. Each substitution is split into onset, nucleus,
  coda and tone. Insertions and deletions are counted separately (tts: 2 insertions; emo: 3 deletions, 3 insertions).
- Distances for the wav errors are a rough measure defined for this report, not a standard metric: onset = number of
  differing articulatory features (place, manner, aspiration); rime = nucleus and coda compared separately; tone = pitch
  distance on a 1-5 scale (T1 55, T2 25, T3 33, T4 21, T5 23, T6 22).
- Training frequency of each gold syllable counts its occurrences in the tts20250516 train split plus the emo train labels
  (2,966,725 syllable tokens).
- Merger pairs are the ones listed in the project notes as colloquial mergers: tone 3/6, 4/6, 2/5 and onset n/l, ng/(none),
  gw/g. An error counts as "merger-only" when every part that differs is one of these pairs.

### Which part is wrong

| | tts | emo |
|---|---|---|
| SER (errors / gold syllables) | 1.55% (143 / 9,250) | 1.71% (228 / 13,344) |
| Substitutions with the onset wrong | 23% (32) | 13% (28) |
| ... the rime wrong | 18% (25) | 16% (36) |
| ... the tone wrong | 65% (92) | 76% (169) |
| Substitutions wrong in exactly one part | 95% | 96% |
| Part wrong as a share of all syllables: onset / rime / tone | 0.35% / 0.27% / 0.99% | 0.21% / 0.27% / 1.27% |

### Tone

| Gold tone | T1 | T2 | T3 | T4 | T5 | T6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Error rate, tts | 0.2% | 0.9% | 1.8% | 0.6% | 1.2% | 1.4% |
| Error rate, emo | 0.3% | 2.1% | 1.2% | 1.2% | 1.5% | 1.8% |

- Most common confusions (tts / emo counts): T3->T6 25 / 23, T6->T4 11 / 16, T6->T3 8 / 17, T4->T6 5 / 18, T2->T5 5 / 11,
  T5->T2 4 / 12, T6->T2 5 / 9, T2->T6 2 / 13.
- Merger pairs (3/6, 4/6, 2/5) account for 58 of 92 tone errors on tts (63%) and 97 of 169 on emo (57%).
- 72% (tts) and 66% (emo) of the tone errors are within one pitch level; 10% and 13% are two or more levels apart.
- Open syllables are the most exposed: the tone error rate for vowel/glide-final syllables is 1.3% (tts) and 1.45% (emo).
  Nasal- and stop-final syllables have 0.5-0.6% on tts and 0.8-1.2% on emo.

### Onset

- 32 onset errors on tts, 28 on emo. The dominant pair is n<->l: on tts, gold `l` becomes `n` in 8 of 362 cases (2.2%);
  on emo, gold `n` becomes `l` in 9 of 292 (3.1%) and gold `l` becomes `n` in 6 of 578 (1.0%). That is 31% (tts) and
  54% (emo) of onset errors.
- Smaller items: `ng` dropped (3 of 155 on tts), `p`->`t` (2 of 68), `g`->`gw` (3 of 1,261), `s`->`z` (3 of 986).
  Every other onset is wrong in about 1% or less of its occurrences.

### Rime

- Nucleus errors cluster on e<->a: gold `e` is wrong 0.8% of the time on tts (6 of 9 errors become `a`); on emo gold `a`
  becomes `e` 8 times of 3,128 and gold `e` becomes `i` or `a` 6 times of 1,225. a/aa confusions come next.
- Codas are almost never wrong (10 errors on tts, 11 on emo, no pattern; the most frequent is t->n at 2 of 801).

### Merger pairs

| | tts | emo |
|---|---|---|
| Errors that differ from the gold only by a merger pair | 71 of 143 (50%) | 109 of 228 (48%) |
| SER without them | 0.78% | 0.89% |

Whether these are model errors or the speaker's real pronunciation cannot be told from the labels: they are pseudo-labels
from earlier models with ToJyutping as the reference (see "Label provenance"), and a converter gives the canonical reading
of the text whatever was spoken. Some of the model's "errors" here are probably what was actually said.

### Characters with several readings (particles, 嚟, 在, 為, ...)

A Cantonese speaker (the dataset's reviewer) pointed out that the particle 呢 is not always one phoneme: `ne1` / `ni1` / `nei1`
depend on context and emotion. `particle_analysis.py` measures how far this explains the errors. Each held-out clip's text is
aligned with its label (one syllable per character); the readings each character has in the 300,886 training labels are
counted, and a reading covering at least 3% of the character's uses is "attested". 835 of 5,231 characters have two or more.

| Held-out set | Syllables on multi-reading characters | Their share of the errors | Error rate, multi-reading vs rest | Errors that are another attested reading of the same character | SER strict -> reading-tolerant |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| tts (9,250 syllables) | 19.8% | 63% | 4.85% vs 0.70% | 81 of 141 (57%) | 1.52% -> **0.65%** |
| emo (13,344 syllables) | 21.4% | 64% | 5.08% vs 0.76% | 163 of 225 (72%) | 1.69% -> **0.46%** |

- The labellers are close to coin-flips on the characters with most errors: 呢 `ne1` 59%, `nei1` 28%, `ni1` 11%, `le1` 3%;
  嚟 `lei4` 54% / `lai4` 46%; 在 `zoi6` 63% / `zoi3` 35%; 為 `wai4` 53% / `wai6` 47%; 到 `dou3` 80% / `dou2` 20%; 你 `nei5` 91% /
  `lei5` 9%; 國 `gwok3` 80% / `gok3` 20%. For these the model's "error" is one acceptable reading where the label has another.
- This is larger than the merger-pair set (tolerant SER 0.78% / 0.89%) and covers the particle readings too. It also matches
  the listening check: four of the six clips where the model matched the audio and the label did not were `lai4`/`lei4`,
  `ne1`/`ni1`, `dou3`/`dou2` and `haa6`/`haa5`.
- The 3% threshold is generous: some attested readings are probably labeller mistakes, so 0.46-0.65% is a lower bound on the
  remaining error, not an exact figure. A reviewer-curated list of the characters that are truly variable would make it exact.
- Implication: report a reading-tolerant SER next to the strict one, and treat the ~1.5% strict error as mostly irreducible
  label variation; the audio cannot decide a coin-flip label.

### Tone mergers in Hong Kong Cantonese: the literature and our data

The three tone pairs are documented, ongoing mergers, not just label conventions. Mok & Wong (2010), "Production and
perception of the rising tones in Hong Kong Cantonese" (CUHK, monosyllables in a carrier phrase), screened 129 speakers
and recruited 17 potential mergers: 9 merged T2/T5, 9 merged T3/T6 and 3 merged T4/T6 (some two pairs). What they report
that matters here:

- The tone space is crowded in the lower pitch range. T2 and T5 share a starting point (T2 rises higher), T3 and T6 differ
  by only about 30 Hz for the female speaker they plot, and T6 and T4 differ only in a slight fall at the end of T4. So
  these pairs are acoustically close and, in isolation, confusable for listeners too.
- Merging is not categorical. Speakers kept both tones in production and perception (the T2/T5 pair above 90% accurate)
  but slower and less accurate than controls, produced intermediate tokens, merged in either direction, and differed a lot
  from speaker to speaker. The process is still in progress.
- In two speakers studied in detail, high-frequency words merged more often.

This matches our data: the model's tone errors and the label-versus-converter deviations are exactly T3/T6, T4/T6 and
T2/T5, and a pitch classifier cannot separate T3 from T6 (66%). It means these "errors" include real variation in how
people speak, which canonical labels cannot record. `tone_merger_patterns.py` checks three predictions on the model's
held-out merger-pair tone errors (0.59% of tts syllables, 0.69% of emo):

| Check | tts held-out | emo held-out |
|---|---|---|
| Direction, gold -> model (reverse in brackets) | T3->T6 24 (6), T6->T4 11 (5), T2->T5 5 (4) | T3->T6 22 (17), T4->T6 16 (14), T5->T2 12 (11) |
| Clips with 2 or more such errors (expected at random) | 2 (1.5), p = 0.45 | 8 (4.2), p = 0.04 |
| Error rate, syllables seen <100 / 100-1k / 1k-10k / >10k times | 3.6 / 2.3 / 0.58 / 0.30% | 13.9 / 2.3 / 0.37 / 0.33% |

- The direction is lopsided on tts (gold T3 heard as T6 four times as often as the reverse) and balanced on emo.
- There is little within-clip clustering (none on tts, borderline on emo), so the data give no clear sign of speakers who
  merge consistently within an utterance; clips are short, so the power is low. Per-speaker analysis is not possible: the
  tts speaker embeddings are per clip and do not form clear speaker clusters (no plateau in the cluster count), and
  neither dataset has speaker ids.
- The error rate falls with how often the syllable occurs in training, the opposite of the paper's frequency effect. The
  two are not comparable (the paper counts word frequency in speakers who merge; our rate also contains the model's
  uncertainty on syllables it has seen few times), so this neither supports nor contradicts it.
- Implications: report a merger-tolerant SER next to the strict one; a word-level (lexical) model is the principled way
  to resolve these pairs, as listeners do, and both datasets carry the Chinese `text` that could supervise it; and whether
  the output should be the canonical or the as-spoken tone is a product decision.

Table 3 of the same paper is a useful reference for how tone should be classified. For two female speakers (the most
confusing of the 17 potential mergers) it codes every T2 and T5 token as produced as the target, as the partner tone, or
"between the two tones". T2 was produced as T5 in 14% of 90 tokens (3.7-22% by speaker and word frequency) and between the
two in 2%; T5 was produced as T2 in 4% of 72 tokens, between the two in 4%, and as T3/T6 in 3%. So even for speakers who
merge, 78-100% of tokens keep the target tone and the rest are a graded mixture of partner and intermediate productions,
not a categorical swap. `tone_ambiguity.py` applies the same three-way coding to the model's tone posteriors (at the
rime's peak frame) for syllables whose gold tone belongs to a merger pair:

| Gold tone -> partner | tts held-out: gold / between / partner | emo held-out: gold / between / partner |
|---|---|---|
| T2 -> T5 | 99.4% / 0.4% / 0.2% | 98.6% / 0.9% / 0.5% |
| T3 -> T6 | 97.9% / 1.4% / 0.6% | 98.2% / 1.2% / 0.6% |
| T4 -> T6 | 99.1% / 0.9% / 0.0% | 98.4% / 1.2% / 0.4% |
| T5 -> T2 | 98.9% / 1.0% / 0.1% | 98.5% / 1.0% / 0.6% |
| T6 -> T3 | 99.2% / 0.8% / 0.0% | 98.7% / 1.0% / 0.3% |
| T6 -> T4 | 98.8% / 1.0% / 0.1% | 98.6% / 1.1% / 0.4% |

- The model is almost fully categorical on these pairs: 98-99% of the syllables have the gold tone with posterior share
  0.9 or more, about 1% sit between, and under 1% go to the partner. The paper's merging speakers produce the partner
  tone in 4-14% of tokens and an intermediate tone in 2-4%; the model's rates are 10 to 30 times lower, as expected if
  only a minority of the datasets' speakers merge.
- The model was trained on one-hot canonical labels, and a CTC posterior at the spike frame is close to saturated, so it
  cannot express the graded realisations the paper describes. That also explains why a language model barely changes the
  choice (next section): there is little uncertainty for it to act on.
- Implication for classification: score or train with graded targets for these pairs (a small share of the mass on the
  partner tone, of the order of a few percent, with a "between" category) instead of one-hot canonical labels. This has not
  been tried.

#### Experiment: graded (merger-aware) targets (not supported)

`compare_heads.py --merger_smoothing EPS` adds, at every frame where the model emits a rime+tone token, a loss term that
keeps probability on the merger-partner tones (2/5, 3/6, 4/6), so the posterior can express "between two tones" instead of
a one-hot choice. Five light runs from the published encoder (6,000 clips, 1,000 steps, same seed; `run_ms.sh`,
`merger_smoothing_report.py`):

| Weight | tts errors (1,000 held-out clips) | vs baseline [95% CI] | emo errors | Median partner share (T2-T5) | Tone-error detection AUROC |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 (one-hot) | 270 (2.92%) | - | 229 (1.72%) | 0.0% | 0.958 |
| 0.005 | 305 (3.30%) | +35 [+8, +61] | 231 | 0.7% | 0.923 |
| 0.015 | 305 (3.30%) | +35 [+6, +61] | 238 | 3.4% | 0.901 |
| 0.05 | 306 (3.31%) | +36 [+9, +64] | 233 | 14.0% | 0.877 |
| 0.15 | 363 (3.92%) | +93 [+64, +124] | 292 (2.19%) | 34.5% | 0.879 |

- Every smoothed run is worse on tts than the one-hot baseline (+35 errors even at the lightest weight), and the posterior
  becomes a worse detector of its own tone errors (AUROC 0.96 -> 0.88-0.92). The merger-pair tone errors also rise
  (78 -> 86-140 on tts).
- The graded posterior does give the lexical decoder more to work with (it removes 23-34 tts-test errors on the smoothed
  models against 13 on the baseline), but baseline + decoder (120 errors on tts test) still beats every smoothed model +
  decoder (121-144).
- The three lightest weights give the same tts error (305-306) although the partner share runs from 0.7% to 14%, so part
  of the gap is run-to-run noise (single seed) rather than a dose effect; the direction is consistent.
- Conclusion: with these labels, graded targets for the merger pairs cost accuracy and buy nothing a reranker can use.
  A graded target would need graded labels (listeners' judgements), not a loss term that spreads probability.

#### Synthetic data and augmentation (light runs)

The JyutVoice TTS (`~/projects/notebooks/notebooks/bert-vits2/frankenstein-matcha2`, jyutping input, speaker embedding from a
reference clip) can say any syllable in any context with an exact label. Samples were checked by ear (good). Voices: 64 clips
sampled from the training corpus; the 6 labelled wavs were NOT used as training voices (their speaker would leak into the only
real-speech test). 5 diffusion steps (0.23 s per clip; ASR intelligibility unchanged from 10 steps; bf16 was slower).

Weak-case map from 3,777 synthetic clips (45,671 syllables; `analyze_synth_weak.py`, run21): SER 6.0% on this rare-heavy
set. Error rate by training uses of the gold syllable: < 100 uses 32%, 100-1k 9%, 1k-10k 3.4%, >= 10k 1.6% (real held-out 0.6%).
Weakest parts: onset n->l (15.8% of gold `n`; many syllables never come out as `n`), null onset -> `ng` (9.3%), `s`->`z`
(3.8%), coda `m`->`n` (4.6%), codas `t`/`p`->`k`, tone T3 6.2% (to T6), T5 5.7%, T6 4.5% (to T4), T3->T1. Sentence-initial
syllables fail 19% (a TTS artifact: initial consonants are weakly articulated, no clipped start). The dictionary lists 2,508
toned syllables but speech uses about 1,300; the 735 dictionary syllables never seen in training are mostly colloquial variant
readings (e.g. `aa4` for 牙), not worth synthesizing.

Light runs from the published encoder (6,000 real clips + extra clips, 1,000 steps, errors on 1,000 real tts clips / 1,035 emo
clips / 79 wav syllables / real syllables used < 1,000 times, 2,147 in the held-out sets). Two seeds (42 / 7) where run:

| Extra training clips | tts | emo | wavs | Real rare syll. | Rare synthetic sentences |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| none (baseline) | 270 / 290 | 229 / 213 | 18 / 21 | 171 / 159 | 18.1% / 18.0% |
| 3,777 generic natural synthetic | 336 / 315 | 244 / 229 | 20 / 28 | 146 / 134 | 13.1% / 12.5% |
| 3,777 generic, augmented | 318 | 229 | 26 | 147 | 13.2% |
| **1,500 rare-heavy natural synthetic** | **298 / 304** | **220 / 233** | **22 / 22** | **141 / 149** | 14.1% / 14.4% |
| 1,500 rare-heavy, augmented | 291 | 249 | 22 | 153 | 14.7% |
| 2,073 generated (masked-LM) rare sentences | 336 | 274 | 21 | 172 | 9.9% |
| 2,073 generated, augmented | 307 | 256 | 21 | 166 | 10.5% |
| 3,777 more REAL clips (control) | 300 | 244 | 17 | 180 | 18.6% |
| 3,777 real clips, augmented (control) | 286 | 222 | 18 | 165 | 18.0% |

- **Run-to-run noise** (baseline seed 42 vs 7): about +-20 errors on tts and emo, +-12 on real rare syllables, +-3 on the
  wavs. Differences smaller than that are not effects.
- **Natural synthetic sentences help the targeted weakness**: real rare-syllable errors fall about 10-16% in both seeds. The
  generic 3,777-clip pool costs a few percent elsewhere (tts +25 to +46, wavs +2 to +7); the **1,500 rare-heavy subset keeps
  most of the gain with no cost beyond the noise**.
- **The synthetic label is exact only for the phone string given to the TTS, not for what it says.** A reviewer heard `ne1`
  spoken as `ni1` in a TTS clip: the TTS was trained on the same pseudo-labelled corpus (tts20250516 carries its training
  features), so it inherits the labels' reading noise for variable-reading characters. In the synthetic pool, 22.6% of the
  syllables sit on multi-reading characters; run21 disagrees with the TTS input on 11.0% of them against 4.6% elsewhere, and
  a round trip cannot catch it (for 呢 run21 wrote the requested reading 97% of the time because it outputs its prior, `ne1`,
  not because the audio says so). Remedy: build synthetic sentences only from characters used with a reliable reading
  (`select_synth_sentences.py --reliable 0.85`: every character's reading covers >= 85% of its training uses; 38% of the
  candidate sentences pass, 18% of the already-synthesized pool).
- **Generated (masked-LM) sentences do not help real data** (172 real rare errors, emo +45): they improve only the probe from
  the same generator. Natural sentences work; odd text does not.
- **Augmentation (noise, echo, reverb, +-5% speed, band limiting) adds nothing measurable** here, alone or on top of the synthetic clips.
  Clean synthetic audio is more fragile under it than real audio (2.5% -> 6.9% SER).
- The extra real-tts errors of the synthetic runs are mostly real degradation: 84% sit where the label equals the ToJyutping
  reading, and the growth is in rime and non-merger tone errors, not in the merger pairs or n/l (so not just wrong labels).
  **Verified by ear** (30 real clips where the synthetic-trained model newly disagreed with a canonical label, judged by a
  Cantonese speaker): the label was right in 24 (80%), the model in 6 (20%, 95% interval about 9-37%; `dou3`/`dou2`,
  `haa6`/`haa5`, `sang1`/`san1`, `ne1`/`ni1`, `lai4`/`lei4`, `jan4`/`jan5`), neither in 0. So the rise is mostly real, and about
  one in five such disagreements is a label error.
- **Expected value at full scale is small.** Rare syllables (< 1,000 uses) are about 30-45% of the errors; a 10-15% cut in
  them is a 4-6% cut in all errors (run21 1.53% -> about 1.45%), below what the 500-clip test can resolve. Any benefit for
  real speech cannot be measured with 79 verified wav syllables.

#### Experiment: can lexical context resolve the merger pairs? (not supported)

Listeners resolve T2/T5, T3/T6 and T4/T6 with context, so a model that knows the words might too. `lexical_experiment.py`
tests the cheapest version without retraining, on the 2,031 held-out clips with tone posteriors.

**A. The ceiling: how much of the label does the text alone determine?** A "reader" that knows the true characters and
gives each the syllable the labellers most often gave it (learned from the 300,886 training transcripts, optionally with
the neighbouring characters) is scored against the labels:

| Errors against the label | tts validation | tts test | emo test |
|---|---|---|---|
| ASR model (run21), hears the audio | 1.56% | 1.53% | 1.68% |
| Reader with the true characters, character only | 3.92% | 3.69% | 4.96% |
| Reader with the true characters and neighbours | 1.97% | 2.44% | 3.11% |

Even with the true text and the labellers' own lexicon, the text reproduces the labels worse than the model does. The labels
carry information that the text does not (they came from models that heard the audio), so lexical knowledge alone cannot
explain, or remove, the model's remaining disagreements.

**B. A lexical decoder.** The characters are latent: for each predicted syllable the candidates are the six tones of the
same rime, scored by the model's own tone log-posterior (weight beta), times the characters the labellers read with that
syllable, scored by a character 4-gram LM trained on the training text; a beam search picks the best sequence and the output
is the chosen tone. Beta is tuned on the validation clips (1.0) and applied unchanged:

| Errors (model alone -> decoder) | tts validation | tts test | emo test |
|---|---|---|---|
| All errors | 72 -> 66 | 71 -> 65 | 223 -> 229 |
| Merger-pair tone errors | 26 -> 21 | 29 -> 24 | 91 -> 85 |

- The decoder removes about 11% of the merger-pair tone errors (146 -> 130 over the three sets) but breaks other syllables,
  mostly on emo (12 fixed, 18 broken), so the net change is about zero (366 -> 360 errors; -6 on tts test, +6 on emo).
- The result does not depend on the LM having seen the sentences (13 of 1,031 emo and none of the tts test sentences are in
  its training text).
- Conclusion: **this lexical route is not shown to resolve the merger pairs**; the effect is small and does not carry over to
  emo. It is a weak test (a 4-gram over characters, tone posteriors read at one frame per syllable, no word segmentation, no
  n-best, no joint training), so it does not rule out a text-supervised model trained end to end; but the ceiling in A says
  the labels are not determined by the text either, which makes large gains from text unlikely.

### Label provenance check: labels versus ToJyutping(text)

Both datasets carry the Chinese `text`. `check_labels_vs_tojyutping.py` converts it with ToJyutping 3.2.0 and compares the
result, syllable by syllable, with the label (all 301,886 clips), then splits the model's held-out errors by whether the
label agrees with the converter.

| Split | Clips | Sentences identical to ToJyutping(text) | Syllables agreeing |
|---|---|---|---|
| tts train | 260,524 | 75.6% | 96.97% |
| tts validation / test | 500 / 500 | 76.4% / 74.8% | 97.19% / 96.93% |
| emo train | 40,362 | 52.7% | 94.81% |
| emo test | 1,035 | 53.6% | 94.96% |

- **Every label has exactly the syllable count of the converter output** (100% of the 301,886 clips), so the labels follow
  the text; they are not free recognition output. That fits labels derived from the text or constrained by it, but it does
  not say whether the audio was used (the dataset owner described the labelling as models with ToJyutping as reference).
- **The label deviations are the colloquial and polyphone variants**, and they are mostly tone: 64% (tts) and 80% (emo) of
  the differing syllables differ in tone. The commonest label -> converter pairs on tts are `nei1`->`ni1` (呢), `lei4`->`lai4`,
  `gok3`->`gwok3`, `dou2`->`dou3`, `zoi3`->`zoi6`, `wai4`->`wai6`; on emo `wai6`->`wai4`, `wui5`->`wui6`, `gam2`->`gam3`,
  `gei2`->`gei1`, `zung2`->`cung4`. The two datasets deviate in different words, which supports two different labelling
  pipelines. Many of these pairs are the merger pairs the model gets wrong.
- **Half of the model's errors are on positions where the label deviates from the converter.** On held-out syllables:

| | tts held-out | emo held-out |
|---|---|---|
| Label differs from the converter | 272 of 9,250 (2.9%) | 672 of 13,341 (5.0%) |
| ... model reproduces the label | 203 (75%) | 554 (82%) |
| ... model errs and sides with the converter | 61 (22%) | 105 (16%) |
| ... model differs from both | 8 | 13 |
| Label equals the converter, model errs | 72 | 104 |
| All model errors | 141 | 222 |

  So 49% (tts) and 53% (emo) of the errors are places where the label is a non-canonical reading and the model gives the
  canonical one. The model has learned most of the labellers' deviations (75-82%) but not all. Of the merger-pair-only
  errors, 58% (tts) and 50% (emo) are of this kind; the rest are places where the label is canonical and the model gives a
  colloquial variant. Whether the label or the model is closer to what was spoken needs the audio.

### Pitch check of the tone disagreements

Which side is closer to the audio when the model and the label disagree only in tone? `check_tone_pitch.py` locates each
predicted syllable with the CTC spikes, tracks F0 with Praat (semitones relative to the clip's median) and trains a tone
classifier on the 17,892 control syllables where label, ToJyutping and model all agree (grouped 5-fold cross-validation by
clip). The pitch window was calibrated on those controls: it starts 0.16 s before the rime's CTC spike, which sits at the
end of the rime (a first attempt with the window after the spike gave contours of the wrong shape and a meaningless
result).

Cantonese tones have a height and a contour, and both shift with the neighbouring syllables (coarticulation, tone change,
declination). A classifier that sees only the syllable's own contour reaches 58.6% on six tones. Giving it the neighbours
(their pitch, predicted tones and timing, and the position in the clip) raises that to **69.6%**, so the context-aware
classifier is used below. For a disputed syllable (label tone a, model tone b) the classifier's chance of preferring a when
a is true and of preferring b when b is true are measured separately on the controls (72-94% depending on the direction),
so a classifier that leans towards one tone cannot make that tone look right; q is the maximum-likelihood share of cases
where the model's tone is the acoustically right one.

| Disagreement (tone is the only difference) | Cases | Pitch favours the model | Estimated share where the model is right (q) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: label deviates from ToJyutping, model sides with ToJyutping | 95 | 66% | **73% (95% interval 59-87%)** |
| B: label = ToJyutping, model deviates | 83 | 52% | 52% (36-66%) |
| A + B | 178 | 60% | 63% (54-73%) |

- **Where the label deviates from the converter and the model gives the canonical tone (A), the audio mostly sides with
  the model:** about 73% (interval 59-87%). Those label deviations are right in only about a quarter of the cases, so
  much of this part of the "error" is label noise.
- **Where the label is the canonical reading and the model gives another tone (B), pitch cannot tell:** 52% (36-66%).
  The model's non-canonical tones are about as often right as wrong, which is what colloquial speech would produce.
- **Overall about 63% (54-73%) of the checkable tone disagreements favour the model (but see the validation below: this
  is probably overstated).** By pair (label tone to model tone: cases, pitch favours the model): T3 to T6 31, 71%; T6 to T4 20, 75%; T4 to
  T6 19, 53%; T6 to T3 15, 47%; T5 to T2 13, 69%; T2 to T6 11, 100%.
- **A tone-3 pitch anchor did not help.** Tones are heard relative to a reference level, usually T3 (T1, T3 and T6 are
  level tones that differ only in height; T2 and T5 share a contour at different heights). Expressing every pitch relative to
  the clip's other predicted-T3 syllables (the syllable itself left out) gives 68.1% on six tones against 69.6% with the clip
  median (70.3% against 68.6% on the syllables that have another T3). T5 stays at about 29% either way. The idea is sound
  phonetically, but the anchor measured from a few short T3 syllables is noisy and its level drifts through the utterance.
- **The context-free classifier gave a different split** (A 59%, B 60%) because its accuracy was lower and it leaned
  towards some tones; the context-aware result replaces it.
- **Limits.** The classifier knows only a syllable's own contour and its neighbours' pitch and predicted tones; it does
  not model how tone varies in continuous speech (changed tones, sentence-final particles, intonation, how a tone is
  realised after different neighbours), which is where labels and model disagree most, and it was trained on the easy,
  canonical syllables. It is better than the context-free version but still imperfect (69.6%), and the correction assumes it is as accurate on the
  disputed syllables as on the controls, which may be optimistic for exactly the colloquial cases. The controls' tones
  come from the same pseudo-labels, so the true accuracy is probably a little higher than measured. Only tone-only
  disagreements are covered (178 of the 363 held-out model errors have a usable contour and differ only in tone); n/l,
  gw/g and e/a need listening. The checked subset is not a random sample of the errors. Per-pair counts are at most 31.

#### Validation on the person-verified wavs: the pitch check is biased towards the model

The 6 wavs (79 syllables) have tones checked by a person against the audio, so they show whether the pitch classifier can
be trusted as a judge (`check_tone_pitch.py wavs`). 73 of the 79 syllables have a usable contour.

| | Pitch classifier | ASR model (run21) |
|---|---|---|
| Tone accuracy against the verified labels | 73% (95% interval 61-82%) | 93% |
| Same measure in-domain (controls / all held-out) | 69.6% | 98.7% |

- **As a calibrated estimate it transfers to real speech.** When the classifier is confident it is as reliable on the wavs
  as in-domain: 95% correct (18 of 19) at confidence 0.9 or more against 96% on the held-out controls; 90% against 88% at
  0.7 or more.
- **As a judge of the model's errors it fails.** The wavs have 5 syllables where the model's tone differs from the
  verified tone. Pitch sided with the verified label in 1 of the 5 and with the model in 4 (for example `soeng6`->`soeng4`:
  the classifier says T4 with probability 0.84; `jyu1`->`jiu3`: T3 with 0.73). Given the classifier's accuracy on the
  controls it should have sided with the truth in about 4 of 5; seeing 1 or fewer has a probability of roughly 1-3% (at
  70-80% accuracy). Its top choice is wrong on all 5 of these syllables: it is wrong where the model is wrong, because both
  read the same pitch evidence (a T6 realised with falling pitch looks like T4 to both).
- **So the correction used above is too optimistic.** The estimate of how often the model is right assumes the classifier
  is as accurate on the disputed syllables as on the controls; here it is not, and its mistakes lean towards the model, so
  the 73% / 52% / 63% most likely overstate how often the model is right. This rests on 5 cases, and the verified labels
  of these tone-test clips may describe the intended tone rather than the realised one, so it is a warning, not a
  measurement. The reranking result is unaffected: it uses no adjudication.
- **What it means for using pitch as a label:** use it only as a confident estimate (probability 0.9 or more, about a
  quarter of syllables, about 95% right) and never to overrule the model or the label on a disputed syllable. Verifying a
  sample of the disputed syllables by ear would give both the real answer and a calibration for any pitch method.

#### Pitch as a reranker: tested, no gain

`check_tone_pitch.py fusion` multiplies, for every predicted syllable, the model's own tone posterior (same rime, all six
tones, at the rime's peak frame) by the pitch classifier's tone probability (out-of-fold, context-aware) and takes the
best tone. The weight of the pitch classifier (gamma) is tuned on the validation clips and applied unchanged to the test
split and the emo test.

| gamma | tts_test errors (model alone 59) | emo_test errors (model alone 166) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 (tuned on validation) | 58 (-1) | 166 (0) |
| 0.25 | 56 (-3) | 165 (-1) |
| 0.5 | 56 (-3) | 170 (+4) |
| 1.0 | 60 (+1) | 180 (+14) |
| 2.0 | 75 (+16) | 216 (+50) |

- At the tuned weight the tone changes in 1 of 4,110 test syllables and 6 of 10,394 emo syllables (3 fixed, 3 broken):
  there is nothing to gain. A heavier weight makes the output follow the pitch classifier, which disagrees with the labels
  more often than the model does.
- Why: the model's tone choice is already far more accurate than a separate pitch classifier (about 98.7% against 69.6% on
  the control syllables, and the model uses the pitch itself); and the tone disagreements that remain are mostly ones where
  the label follows a convention the audio does not determine (the audio sides with the model in 73% of the cases where the
  label deviates from ToJyutping, and cannot tell in the rest). Moving towards the acoustics does not move towards the labels.
- A perfect tone chooser could fix 33 (validation), 40 (test) and 116 (emo) tone-only errors, so there is headroom, but this
  pitch evidence cannot find it.
- Pitch as a training aid (an F0 input or an auxiliary contour target) is not tested. The `--use_f0` option in the code is
  crude (z-scored absolute F0 added to the encoder output through one linear layer, no contour or neighbour context) and has
  a known loading bug. By the same argument the in-domain gain should be small; a possible benefit is on real speech,
  which needs a larger labelled real-speech set to measure.

### Are the errors consistent across datasets? (overfitting check)

A dataset-level SER can hide a model that fits one dataset's conventions. `error_consistency.py` decodes each model on
the held-out tts clips (validation + test, 9,250 syllables), the emo test (13,344), and 1,000 randomly sampled training
clips of each dataset, then compares the share of syllables with a wrong onset, rime or tone (95% clip-bootstrap
intervals). Models: run21 (trained on both datasets), run20 (tts only, so emo is unseen) and the published model (emo
only, so tts is unseen).

| Model | Dataset | Status | SER | Onset | Rime | Tone |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| run21 | tts held-out | seen domain | 1.55% | 0.35% | 0.27% | 0.99% |
| run21 | emo held-out | seen domain | 1.71% | 0.21% | 0.27% | 1.27% |
| run21 | tts train sample | trained on | 1.06% | 0.17% | 0.16% | 0.78% |
| run21 | emo train sample | trained on | 1.49% | 0.11% | 0.31% | 1.09% |
| run20 | tts held-out | seen domain | 1.51% | 0.34% | 0.28% | 0.92% |
| run20 | emo held-out | **unseen** | 3.05% | 0.37% | 0.57% | 2.26% |
| published | tts held-out | **unseen** | 5.49% | 1.25% | 1.49% | 3.19% |
| published | emo held-out | seen domain | 0.01% | 0.00% | 0.00% | 0.01% |

- **run21 shows no sign of fitting one dataset.** Its held-out rates agree across tts and emo: none of the part-rate
  differences is significant (onset +0.14 points [-0.02, +0.27], tone -0.27 [-0.56, +0.01]), no token has a clearly
  different error rate (Fisher p < 0.01), and the tone confusions are the same pairs in both (cosine similarity 0.86;
  98-99% of the errors fall into pairs seen in the other dataset). The only difference is a mild shift in which part
  fails: tone is 64% of single-part substitutions on tts and 75% on emo (chi-square p = 0.02).
- **The train-versus-held-out gap is small.** On its own training clips run21 still makes 1.06% (tts) and 1.49% (emo)
  errors, against 1.55% and 1.71% held-out. Only the tts onset gap is significant (0.17% to 0.35%). 42-48% of the
  errors on training clips are merger pairs, so the roughly 1% floor is label ambiguity, not memorisation.
- **The dataset-dependent part is tone, then rime; onset is not.** run20 on the unseen emo data goes from 1.51% to 3.05%
  SER: onset unchanged (0.34% to 0.37%), rime 2.1 times higher, tone 2.5 times higher. The extra tone errors are the gold
  tones 6, 4, 5 and 2 (2-4 times as often on emo, Fisher p < 0.01); tones 1 and 3 do not change, and 3->6 is as common
  on tts as on emo. Training on emo too removes the gap (run21 emo tone 2.26% to 1.27%).
- **A single-dataset model is clearly dataset-specific.** The published model on the unseen tts data degrades everywhere
  (onset 3.6 times, rime 5.5 times, tone 3.2 times); 18 of its 25 gold-`g` errors are `g`->`gw`, and it drops final stops
  and glides. That `g`/`gw` pattern points at a labelling-convention difference between the two corpora.
- **The 6 wavs have a different failure mix from both datasets.** Onset errors are 7 of the 12 wav errors, against
  13-23% of substitutions in the datasets, and `m`->`b`, `d`->`z`, `p`->`b` occur in neither dataset. A third domain
  therefore does not behave like either of the two, which is the real overfitting risk. It rests on 12 errors.
- **Caveats.** The published model has 0.00-0.02% error on both its emo test and an emo training sample, so that test is
  effectively seen and says nothing about generalisation; run21's emo held-out is not clean either, because its encoder
  comes from that model. Given the label provenance, this 0.00% may also be agreement with the labeller: if the emo labels
  came from the published model or a close relative, it matches them by construction (not verified). The same applies to the
  dataset differences above: "dataset-dependent tone and rime" may be differences between the two labelling pipelines
  (the `g`/`gw` pattern fits that), not only differences in the audio. The training samples are 1,000 clips each, the emo
  copy used is the local one, and the Fisher tests are not corrected for the number of tokens tested.

## 4. What this suggests

1. **Decide how the merger pairs are handled.** Half the in-domain error is there. Options: relabel (or double-label) the
   colloquial readings, or report a merger-aware SER next to the strict one. More data of the same kind will not remove it.
2. **Cover rare syllables.** Frequency-aware sampling, or corpora with more of the under-1,000-occurrence syllables.
   This should help the largest remaining block of non-merger errors.
3. **Close the real-speech gap with real data.** Build a larger evaluation set (hundreds of syllables, labels checked against
   the audio) before drawing conclusions; then try SpecAugment (`mask_time_prob` is still 0), speed and noise augmentation,
   and real-speech corpora. The wav errors are of kinds the held-out sets never show, so more in-domain training will
   likely not fix them.
4. **Lexical rescoring was tested and is not a reliable fix** (section 4.1). The right answer is almost always in the
   n-best, so a better reranker could still pay off, but the syllable n-gram LM does not get it.
5. **Not worth pursuing:** a different optimizer (fused AdamW is about 9% of a training step; a step is compute-bound at
   about 42% of the 3090's peak), and further head-design work (single head already decided).

### 4.1 Post-processing test: n-best rescoring with a syllable LM

Setup: CTC prefix beam search (beam 64, 34 distinct syllable sequences per clip on average) on checkpoint-40000's
per-frame probabilities, rescored with `CTC log-probability + alpha * LM log-probability + beta * length`. The LM is an
interpolated Kneser-Ney syllable n-gram (order 3 or 4) trained on the tts and emo training transcripts (300,886 sentences,
2.97 M syllables). `alpha`, `beta` and the order were tuned on the validation split only (160 settings), then applied
unchanged to the test split, the emo test and the wavs.

| | Errors, greedy | Errors, rescored | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| tts validation (tuned on it) | 72 | 55 | in-sample, not evidence |
| tts test | 71 | 64 (-9%) | 95% bootstrap CI -0.35 to +0.04 points; 12 clips better, 6 worse |
| emo test | 228 | 237 (+4%) | 17 clips better, 26 worse |
| 6 wavs | 12 | 10 | both fixes are in one clip (`baa1`->`maa1`, `zou1`->`dou1`) |

- A 5-fold cross-validation over all 2,035 held-out clips (tune on four fifths, apply to one fifth) goes from 371 to 364
  errors (-2%). Across settings the tts sets gain 5-25%, but the emo test gains at most 1% (alpha 0.1) and loses at larger
  alphas.
- The oracle over the n-best is 0.1% SER (13 errors on emo, 5 on tts test) against 1.5-1.7% for greedy decoding: a perfect
  chooser would almost eliminate the errors, and the LM does not choose well.
- By error type (test and emo pooled), merger-pair-only errors stay at 146 and tone-only errors rise from 84 to 89;
  onset-only errors fall from 9 to 6. The LM cannot settle tone and n/l questions the audio leaves open.
- The earlier claim that this kind of rescoring removes about 30% of in-domain errors came from tuning on the test split
  and does not hold up.
- Only 2% of the tts held-out sentences (and 15% of the emo ones) appear verbatim in the training transcripts, so this is
  not leakage; those clips have almost no errors with or without the LM.

Conclusion: do not enable the LM by default. It is cheap to keep as an option, but it will not remove the merger-pair
errors or the tone errors, which are most of the problem.

## 6. Limits of this analysis

- **Label provenance (from the dataset owner): both datasets were labelled by previously trained models with ToJyutping as
  the reference, not by human annotators.** Consequences: SER is agreement with that labelling; where the speaker deviates
  from the canonical reading the label can be wrong while the model is right; mistakes shared with the labelling models are
  invisible in the SER; and a model trained on these labels learns the labellers' conventions as well as the language.
  Both datasets also carry the Chinese `text` that ToJyutping reads; how close the labels are to it is measured in
  section 2, and a pitch check of the tone disagreements is there too. Not yet done: listening to the disagreements pitch
  cannot judge (onset n/l, gw/g, nucleus e/a).
